# The Slop Catalogue

SKILL.md names the four big faces of generated design. This file goes further: every other default you will meet, what each one looks like in a real section or screen, what to put there instead, and a set of quick tests you can run on a finished page. Read it when you are listing the slop in step 3 of the workflow, and again before you call the work done.

## How to use this file

Work through a page and write down each tell you find, with the element it lives on. A tell is only a problem when it was left there by default. A pill badge announcing a real product launch on its actual launch week is a decision. The same pill above every hero is a habit. When you find one, ask what this specific product needs in that spot, and answer from the product.

## More faces beyond the big four

These show up less often than the faces in SKILL.md, but each one is a recognisable template.

**The chat-first AI product.** A centred input box on an empty page, a row of suggestion chips below it ("Draft an email", "Summarise a document"), a sparkle icon next to anything intelligent, and a soft gradient halo around the input. It tells the visitor nothing about what the product knows or who it is for. Show a real conversation that happened, with real output, and name the job ("Reads your supplier invoices and flags the ones that changed price").

**The local-business template.** A full-width stock photo with a dark overlay, a white headline ("Welcome to Adaeze's Kitchen"), a gold "Book now" button, a row of three round icons (quality, service, value), a five-star badge with no source, and an embedded map at the bottom. The fix is the shop's own photos, the actual menu or price list on the page, opening hours near the top, and a phone number or WhatsApp link that works with one tap.

**The shop theme.** A product grid of identical white cards, a "Sale" ribbon on random items, a newsletter popup within three seconds, and a mega footer. Give the range an idea of its own: sorted by how people shop (by occasion, by size, by price) with real photographs shot the same way.

**The developer portfolio as a terminal.** A fake command prompt, a typing animation, green text on black, and a list of skills shown as progress bars. Skill bars carry no information. Show three projects with what you did, what changed as a result, and a link someone can open.

**The conference or event page.** A countdown timer in four boxes, a gradient banner, speaker cards with identical grey silhouettes, and a ticket button repeated six times. The visitor wants date, place, price, who is speaking and how to get there. Put those in that order, in text, near the top.

## The overcorrection, in more detail

SKILL.md describes it in a paragraph. In practice you can spot it by a cluster of choices that all say "I know about slop": an off-white page, headline type at 120px or larger, small uppercase monospace labels with section numbers, coordinates, or a ticking clock in the header, thin rules between every section, a single rust or olive accent, and an absence of photographs. The second version of the same reflex is the concept that borrows a transport timetable, a departure board or a ledger sheet, because those look "designed". They are fine ideas once. Used as the answer to every brief they become the new template.

What to do when you notice it:

- Put a photograph, illustration, strong colour field or the real product on the first screen.
- Allow one loud decision (a large flat colour, a heavy typeface, an oversized image) instead of a page made entirely of restraint.
- Check the concept sentence. If it came from the first place your mind went, replace it with the third idea.

Taking things away does not produce design. A page stripped of gradients, cards, icons and colour ends up as an empty frame with a good typeface. Specific choices are the goal, and restraint is one tool among several. Plenty of memorable sites are loud, colourful and full of pictures.

## Section by section (sites)

| Section | The tell | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Nav | Logo left, five links, a "Sign in" text link and a dark button, translucent blur background | Fewer links, named for what the visitor wants ("Menu", "Prices", "Find us"). Solid background unless the blur has a reason. |
| Hero headline | Centred, 5 to 9 words, two short sentences, last words in an accent | One plain sentence that says what the thing is and who it helps. Left-aligned is allowed. |
| Hero media | A browser frame around a screenshot, tilted or floating | The real product at a readable size, a real photo, or a drawing in one consistent hand. |
| Hero buttons | A solid button plus a ghost button | One action. A second route, if needed, is a text link. |
| Trust strip | Greyscale logos of companies that never signed up | Real names with permission, or one real quote, or nothing. |
| Features | Three or six cards with icon, bold title, one line | Show one feature working. If you list, use a plain list with the verb first. |
| How it works | Numbered 1, 2, 3 with circles joined by a line | Only when order matters. Otherwise tell it as a short story with a picture of each moment. |
| Stats | Four giant numbers with small captions | One number inside a sentence, with its source and date. |
| Testimonials | A grid of cards, avatar, five stars, vague praise | One quote, specific, with a real name, place and photo, or none. |
| Pricing | Three tiers, the middle one highlighted | Show the plans that exist. If there is one price, say it high on the page. |
| FAQ | A long accordion of questions nobody asked | Answer the real objections near the place they arise. Keep a handful of actual questions from your inbox. |
| Closing band | "Ready to transform your business?" with a button | End with the last useful facts: price, address, hours, how to start. |
| Footer | Four columns of links to pages that do not exist | Only the links that lead somewhere, plus contact details and the legal lines you need. |

## Screen by screen (apps)

| Screen | The tell | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Home or dashboard | Row of metric cards, then charts, then a table | Start with what the person must do next: the three overdue invoices, the booking that needs confirming. |
| List or table | Avatar circles, coloured tag pills in every row, hover shadows | Text-first rows, aligned columns, one status word per row, numbers right-aligned in tabular figures. |
| Detail page | Cards stacked with equal weight | One main column for the record and a narrow side column for context. Title, state and the primary action at the top. |
| Forms | One long vertical stack of fields, labels as placeholders | Group by topic, put labels above fields, show format help in advance, mark only the optional fields. |
| Settings | Tabs plus a card per toggle, each with a description | A plain list grouped by topic, switches on the right, most-used first. |
| Chat or AI panel | Sparkle icon, bubbles of two colours, suggested prompts, typing dots | Show what the assistant knows, let people attach the thing they mean, show the result in context rather than as a chat reply only. |
| Onboarding | Three-step wizard with a progress bar and a welcome emoji | Ask only what is needed to show a first useful result. Let people skip, and put the rest in the product. |
| Empty state | A grey illustration and "Nothing here yet" | Say what will appear and give the button that creates the first one. |
| Auth screens | Centred card on a gradient, social buttons stacked | A short form, a plain explanation of what happens next, and one real sentence about the product. |
| Modals and toasts | Every confirmation is a modal, every action a toast with a tick | Confirm only when something is hard to undo, and name the thing ("Delete invoice 0043?"). Let reversible actions offer undo. |
| Mobile web app | The desktop sidebar squashed into a hamburger | Bottom navigation for the three to five main places, thumb-height controls, and the primary action within reach. |

## Small details that give it away

**Buttons.** A pill-shaped black button with a trailing arrow. A "Get started" label. A button with a keyboard hint inside it when the user is on a phone. Name the action and match the corner radius to the rest of the system.

**Icons.** Every icon drawn at the same 24px size, 2px stroke, next to 15px text. Icons in front of every list item. Use fewer, size them to the text, and match stroke weight to the letterforms.

**Radius and shadow.** The same large radius on avatars, buttons, cards and inputs. A shadow with soft 40px blur on every card. Smaller things get smaller radii, and shadow is for objects that sit above the page.

**Numbers and dates.** Round numbers, perfect percentages, and every date being "Today". Real data is uneven: a delivery at 14:07, a price of 4,350 naira, a "last seen 3 days ago".

**Names and places.** John Smith, Jane Doe, Acme Inc, London in every address. Use names and places your audience would recognise: Funmi at Brixton Market, Tunde Bello of Bello & Sons Printing, a Leeds bike repair shop.

**Motion.** Every block fades up as you scroll, every card lifts on hover, a ticker of logos scrolls forever. Animate state changes and one moment that matters.

**Copy.** Words like seamless, effortless, powerful, elevate, unlock, and supercharge. Headlines built as two short opposing sentences. Em dashes in every paragraph. A button that says "Learn more" when it goes to a pricing page.

**Imagery.** Placeholder services, smiling people at laptops, abstract 3D shapes, and the isometric office. If you have no photos, use type, colour, a drawing in one hand, or the product itself.

## Three ways slop slips back in

1. **A component library at its defaults.** shadcn, MUI, Chakra and Bootstrap each have a recognisable look. Change the tokens (colour, radius, type, spacing) before you use a single component, and open the component files to edit where the defaults do not fit.
2. **A template that was "just for the scaffold".** Placeholder text and images get left in. Search for lorem, placehold, picsum, example.com, Acme and your own TODO markers before you finish.
3. **Review by feel.** You glance at the page, it looks fine, and you stop. Fine is the average. Use the tests below so the check has a result.

## Quick tests for a finished page

Run these in order. Each takes under a minute.

1. **Cover the logo.** Could another company in this category put its name here? If yes, the direction has not reached the page.
2. **Swap the category.** Change the product name to a different industry. If the layout, colour and headline construction would still work, it is a template.
3. **Squint.** Blur your eyes or shrink the browser window to a thumbnail. One thing should win on each screen, and it should be the thing that matters.
4. **Greyscale.** Remove colour (in DevTools rendering emulation or with `filter: grayscale(1)` on `html`). Is hierarchy still clear? Can you still tell states apart?
5. **Phone first screen.** Open it at 390 wide. Do you see evidence, a plain statement and the action without scrolling?
6. **Subtract.** Delete one section at a time. If the page is no worse, leave it deleted.
7. **Point at a value.** Choose a colour, a gap or a word at random and say why it is that value. "It looked good" is not a reason.
8. **Read it out loud.** Rewrite any line you would feel odd saying to a customer at a counter.
9. **Count the repeats.** If three or more elements are identical in size, structure and styling, ask whether they should be one list or one feature shown properly.
10. **Tab through it.** Press Tab from the top. Is focus visible on every step, and does the order match the visual order?

## Checks you can run in the browser console

These are rough, and they catch what the eye misses on a long page.

```js
// Distinct font sizes in use (more than about 9 means the scale isn't a scale)
new Set([...document.querySelectorAll('body *')]
  .map(e => getComputedStyle(e).fontSize)).size

// Distinct border radii
new Set([...document.querySelectorAll('body *')]
  .map(e => getComputedStyle(e).borderRadius)
  .filter(r => r !== '0px')).size

// Gradients in use
[...document.querySelectorAll('body *')]
  .filter(e => getComputedStyle(e).backgroundImage.includes('gradient')).length

// Text under 12px
[...document.querySelectorAll('body *')]
  .filter(e => e.childNodes.length && parseFloat(getComputedStyle(e).fontSize) < 12).length
```

A healthy page has six to nine font sizes, two to four radii, close to zero gradients unless the concept calls for one, and no text under 12px. These are signals to look closer, and each has exceptions.

## The last question

If a reviewer screenshots the page, what would they say about it? "Clean" and "modern" are what people say about the average. You want something with a noun in it: "the one with the market-stall signage", "the one that opens on the actual price list". If you cannot imagine a specific description, the page does not have a specific idea yet.
